## 1. What students think we think about AI
Research is emerging showing a gap between staff AI policies and what students actually believe is permitted. The student perspective is often more anxious and confused than we realise. Angle: what are we actually communicating, and to whom?

---

## 2. The privacy question nobody is asking
Staff are putting student feedback, draft grant text, and meeting notes into Copilot. What are Curtin's actual data boundaries? A practical, non-alarmist take on what's fine and what's worth pausing on.

---

## 3. AI didn't kill the essay — it killed the bad essay
Some disciplines are quietly discovering that AI-generated work is easy to spot not because of detection tools, but because it's shallow. The implication: depth and disciplinary rigour matter more, not less.

---

## 4. The 10% problem (promising)
AI is very good at the first 80% of any task. The last 10% — the bit that requires judgement, nuance, or domain knowledge — still needs you. And that's the bit most people aren't thinking about.

Possible angle: The Question Architect framing — when AI makes answers cheap, the scarce skill is knowing how to ask the right question.
- Domain authority: deep knowledge accelerates AI output; without it, AI produces noise
- Inquiry design: framing complex problems into precise, strategic prompts
- Outcome integration: knowing why the answer matters — that judgement can't be automated
- The reframe: the value of expertise is no longer in the answer, it's in the inquiry

Relevant paper: "The homogenizing effect of large language models on human expression and thought"
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2026.01.003

Toolkit resource: Question Architect (ai-toolkit/downloads/question-architect.qmd)

## 5. Teaching AI use to people still learning the field
Strong candidate for the Briefing immediately after Issue #4. Picks up the question Issue #4 deliberately leaves open: once we are not catching cheaters, what new skill are we actually teaching, and how do we teach it to someone who has not yet acquired the judgement they need to evaluate AI output?

Working spine:

- **The asymmetry.** A calculator is deterministic; a novice can trust the output. AI is plausible-not-correct; a novice cannot evaluate the output without already having some of the judgement the assessment is trying to build. That asymmetry is the heart of the problem and is the bit "just teach them to use AI" hand-waves over.
- **The new skill, named clearly.** Not "how to use AI." The skill is "how to use AI while staying in the loop on the thinking" (conversation not delegation). For a novice that translates into structured verification: cross-check against a textbook or trusted source, ask a different question and see if answers cohere, justify in your own words why the output is correct.
- **Scaffolded use, not banned use.** First-year students in a discipline should probably not use AI as an answer machine. They can use it as a question-generator, a counter-argument partner, an explainer of jargon they have not yet built vocabulary for. The legitimate use case grows with the learner.
- **The honest unsolved bit.** The viva / process-review approach in Lane 2 takes more staff time per student than a written exam. The sector has not solved that. Naming it again here keeps the issue from sounding glib.

Addresses fears 3 and 4 named in the Issue #4 editorial conversation (fear of being exposed for not yet having the skill, and real pedagogical confusion about what the skill even is).

Closing-thought candidate: AI literacy is not a tool skill; it is a thinking habit. We teach thinking habits by practising them under guidance, not by handing the tool over.

Toolkit links: Conversation Not Delegation, RTCF, The Conversation Loop, Verification Mindset, Critique Toolkit.

## 6. AI in the trough of disillusionment (business school lens)
Strong candidate for the first Briefing of semester 2 (week 2 or 3). Reframes the AI fatigue staff are sensing as a predictable phase of a normal technology lifecycle rather than as personal disillusionment. Lands well because it uses business school vocabulary the audience already teaches.

Working spine:

- Open by naming what staff are sensing (AI fatigue, "where is the actual value", scepticism of vendor promises) and reframing it through a familiar lifecycle vocabulary
- Use the Gartner Hype Cycle as the entry point because the audience knows it. Then deepen with Carlota Perez's installation/deployment phase distinction, which has more academic spine and tells a richer story: the deployment phase is where the actual economic value gets generated, after the turbulence
- Historical comparison: dot-com bust 2000, then about five to seven years of unglamorous productive work, then cloud, mobile, SaaS, the things that actually changed the world. AI is plausibly in the equivalent moment now
- What changes in the trough: stop doing transformation theatre and vendor evangelism. Start doing narrow integration, harness and scaffolding work, local AI for privacy-sensitive use cases. Boring but valuable
- Speak to SoMM specifically: marketing AI, professional services AI, accounting and finance AI sitting at different points on the cycle. Each sub-discipline has its own trough trajectory
- Closing thought: the trough is where the unglamorous useful work gets done, and that work is exactly what a business school can teach

Pairs with toolkit resources: AI-Last Philosophy (trough thinking), Conversation Not Delegation (the unsexy productive move), the Question Architect (where expertise lives when answers get cheap).

References to consider: current Gartner Hype Cycle for AI, Carlota Perez *Technological Revolutions and Financial Capital*, Rogers *Diffusion of Innovations*.

Closing-line candidate: AI did not skip the trough. It is in it, and the trough is where business schools have always done their best work.

## 7. Detection, Assessment 2030, and the two-lane model
Drafted in May 2026 as the originally planned Briefing #4, then parked. The material is sound but the issue's framing put the editor in an unintended authority posture: explaining institutional policy to colleagues who already had views on it. The "Curtin's AI Position at a Glance" one-pager in the Toolkit now covers the institutional summary side. The detection/redesign argument may still surface usefully in a future issue if there is a specific live moment to peg it to (a TEQSA signal, a Mollie talk, a Turnitin Clarity rollout milestone, a sector report).

Spine if revisited:

- Lead: most know detection is off. The reading that the University has given up is wrong. Detection was the cheap layer. Redesign is the expensive one and it puts more on us, not less.
- Worth reading: detection was a losing game (false-positive and false-negative rates, perverse incentive structure). Catching cheating was always standing in for the harder question of whether learning happened, and the proxy stopped working.
- The redesign, two-lane model: Lane 1 (secure assessments, identity verified, supervised) and Lane 2 (open assessments, GenAI permissible, process is the evidence). Every degree mixes both. The workload cost in Lane 2 is real and worth naming.
- Closing: AI did not break university assessment. It revealed that university assessment had quietly been broken for a long time. What we are doing now is fixing it.

Toolkit companion already in place: `curtin-ai-position` (Curtin's AI Position at a Glance). Existing companion from Issue #1: Assessment AI Stress Test. If revived, link Mollie Dollinger's public Assessment 2030 page and consider a courtesy heads-up before publishing.
