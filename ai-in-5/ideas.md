## Ask the Wrong Question on Purpose
**Theme:** Thinking | **Concept:** Problem framing (Conversation Loop — Brainstorm stage)

**Challenge:** Pick something you're working on today. Before you ask AI for help, deliberately describe your attempted solution instead of your actual problem. See what AI gives you. Now try again — this time describe the problem, not the fix. Compare the two responses. The second one is almost always more useful, and the gap between them shows you how much framing matters.

**Teaching:** You want to "make this rubric clearer." But is your real problem that students don't understand the criteria, or that the criteria don't match what you actually value? Ask AI the real question.

**Research:** You want to "fix the introduction." But is the real problem structure, scope, or that you haven't landed your argument yet? State the actual uncertainty.

**Professional:** You want to "reformat this spreadsheet." But is the real problem that people can't find what they need? Ask AI about the information problem, not the formatting problem.

**Notes:** Best suited for Week 5+ when staff have built some AI comfort. More cerebral than the early weeks — teaches problem framing rather than tool familiarity. Could pair well with the Conversation Loop experience launch.

## Spot the AI
**Theme:** Ethics | **Concept:** Detection tools are unreliable

**Challenge:** Two paragraphs about why Curtin is a great place to work. One was written by a human, one by Copilot. Can you tell which? (A) 'Curtin brings together people who genuinely want to make a difference. The campus buzzes with ideas, the coffee is decent, and there is always someone down the corridor who knows the thing you need to know.' (B) 'Curtin University offers a dynamic and supportive work environment characterised by a strong commitment to innovation, diversity, and professional development opportunities for all staff.' Answer: B is the AI. If you got it wrong — that is exactly why this matters.

**Teaching:** You probably mark student writing. Could you reliably spot AI in an assignment? Detection tools get it wrong 20–40% of the time. Design for learning, not detection.

**Research:** Next time you review a manuscript, notice the writing style. Could sections have been AI-generated? The answer increasingly is: you can't tell, and that changes how we think about authorship.

**Professional:** You write reports, briefs, and proposals. If AI can produce text indistinguishable from yours, what does that mean for how we value writing versus the thinking behind it?

**Notes:** Originally slotted as Week 3. Parked 2026-03-24 to make room for the Two-Chat Workflow challenge. Good standalone challenge — works any time.

## Before You Paste
**Theme:** Ethics | **Concept:** Inside the tenant is not the same as private

**Challenge:** Before your next Copilot prompt, pause and ask: "If a screenshot of this prompt ended up in the wrong inbox, would I be fine with that?" Curtin's Copilot is more contained than the public ChatGPT — your prompts stay inside the Microsoft 365 tenant and aren't used to train the public model — but inside the tenant is not the same as private to you. Student identifiers, unpublished research data, HR detail, anything covered by an NDA or ethics approval: those don't belong in the chat box. The skill isn't paranoia. It's pausing long enough to notice the difference.

**Teaching:** You want Copilot to help draft feedback on a struggling student's work. The instinct is to paste the assignment in. Strip the name and ID first — or better, ask Copilot for the feedback framework and apply it to the work yourself. The pedagogical thinking is the part it can help with; the identifiable student work isn't what it needs.

**Research:** Drafting analysis from interview transcripts? Don't paste raw transcripts in to "help find themes." Either de-identify thoroughly first, or describe the patterns you're seeing and let Copilot help you write about them. Your ethics approval almost certainly has something to say about where that data is allowed to live — and a Copilot chat is probably not on the list.

**Professional:** You're drafting a difficult HR letter or summarising a sensitive complaint. The shortcut is to paste the original in and ask for help. Don't. Describe the situation in general terms, get a template or structure, then fill in the specifics in Word yourself. Same time saved, none of the exposure.

**Notes:** Drafted 2026-05-27. Curtin-specific claim — "prompts stay inside the M365 tenant and aren't used to train the public model" — should be checked against current Curtin IT comms before sending; Copilot data-handling wording shifts. Good as a standalone or as the fourth beat of the ethics arc after Week 12.

## The Model Is Not the Product *(seed — needs fleshing out)*
**Theme:** Thinking | **Concept:** The harness around the model shapes what you experience

**Seed idea:** Captured 2026-05-28. People say "ChatGPT" or "Copilot" as if they're talking about the model — but what they actually use is the *harness*: the chat UI, the system prompt, the tool wiring, the safety layers, the file-upload and web-search affordances, the memory features, the org-level admin policy. Same underlying model can feel very different across harnesses (ChatGPT vs Copilot vs Claude.ai vs Cursor vs an API call). Understanding this helps staff make sense of why "ChatGPT was better last week" or why "Copilot won't do what ChatGPT does" — it's usually the harness, not the model.

**To flesh out:** Need a concrete 5-minute challenge. Maybe: ask the same prompt in two different harnesses (Copilot in Word vs Copilot chat, or Copilot vs ChatGPT free) and notice what's different — not the answer, the *experience*. Affordances, tone, refusals, formatting. Could pair with role lenses (teaching/research/professional) easily.

**Pairs with:** [[what-else-shapes-the-output]] — together they make a mini-arc on "the model is one input among many."

**Cross-ref:** Also captured as a seed in `the-briefing-archive/ideas.md` (#8). Could live as a 5-min challenge, a Briefing issue, or both — decide when fleshing out.

## What Else Shapes the Output *(seed — needs fleshing out)*
**Theme:** Thinking | **Concept:** Context beyond the prompt — config files, memory, persistent instructions

**Seed idea:** Captured 2026-05-28. The prompt isn't the only thing the model sees. Increasingly, output is shaped by: persistent instructions (ChatGPT custom instructions, Claude project instructions, CLAUDE.md-style config files in dev tools), memory across sessions (saved facts, prior conversation summaries), uploaded files acting as context (a CV/resume, a style guide, a policy doc), system prompts set by the org or the app vendor. Two staff using "the same Copilot" can get systematically different answers because of what's been configured upstream. Helps demystify "why does it know that about me?" and "why is it suddenly worse?"

**To flesh out:** Concrete challenge ideas — (a) check your ChatGPT/Copilot custom instructions and notice what's been telling the model how to behave, (b) upload your CV/PD and ask it to draft something "in your voice", compare with no upload, (c) for staff using Copilot in M365, notice that it can see your recent emails/docs — that's context too. The teaching moment: output quality is partly an *information hygiene* problem, not just a prompting problem.

**Pairs with:** [[the-model-is-not-the-product]] — natural follow-up week.

**Cross-ref:** Also captured as a seed in `the-briefing-archive/ideas.md` (#9). Likely meatier than 5 minutes — Briefing may be the better home.

## What's Yours to Keep
**Theme:** Strategy | **Concept:** AI takes tasks, not your judgement (augment, not replace)

**Challenge:** Take two minutes and write two short lists. List one: three things in your working week that AI could genuinely take off your plate — the routine, the repetitive, the first-draft grunt work. List two: three things it couldn't — the parts that need your judgement, your relationships, or your name on the outcome. Most people find the second list slower to write and harder to hand over. That list is the point. AI changes the tasks, not the role — and noticing the difference is how you stay in charge of it rather than worried by it.

**Teaching:** AI can draft feedback scaffolding, summarise a marking guide, or tidy a slide deck. It can't make the judgement call on a borderline student, hold the duty of care when someone's struggling, or decide what's actually worth teaching. Which of those did you train years for?

**Research:** AI can format references, triage a first pass through the literature, or rephrase a clunky paragraph. It can't decide what your finding *means*, defend it to a sceptical reviewer, or stand behind it with your name attached. The interpretation and the accountability stay yours.

**Professional:** AI can draft routine comms, reformat a report, or summarise a long thread. It can't read the room in a tense meeting, hold the institutional memory of why a decision was made, or navigate the politics of who needs to be told what. The human-judgement parts are the parts that were always the job.

**Notes:** Drafted 2026-06-02. Fills the "AI & work / jobs" gap in the content bank — nothing else touches it. Tone is deliberately *augment-not-replace* (constructive, lowers anxiety) rather than naming the replacement fear head-on. Good fit for a Strategy slot. Pairs naturally with [[make-it-argue-back]].

## Make It Argue Back
**Theme:** Strategy | **Concept:** AI as a dialogue partner that sharpens *your* reasoning (not a substitute for it)

**Challenge:** Pick a view you hold or a decision you've half-made. Paste it into Copilot and ask: "Give me the strongest possible case *against* this," then "What am I assuming here that I haven't actually justified?" Whatever it says, don't accept it — push back, defend, refine. The goal isn't a better answer *from* AI. It's a sharper argument *from you*. The evidence on what actually builds critical thinking points to one thing above all: dialogue. AI is a tireless sparring partner you can point at your own thinking any time you like.

**Teaching:** Drafting a stance on an assessment policy, or a decision about AI in your unit? Ask AI for the strongest student objection *and* the strongest colleague objection. Walk into the conversation having already answered both.

**Research:** Before you submit, ask AI to play Reviewer 2 — the harshest *fair* reviewer your work could draw. Where's the weakest link in the argument? Better to hear it now than in the decision letter.

**Professional:** Writing a recommendation or a proposal? Ask "what would a sceptical decision-maker push back on?" The pushback you can answer makes the case stronger; the one you can't is the thing to fix before it goes up.

**Toolkit link:** Pairs with the **Devil's Advocate** technique (the how-to prompt) and the **Abrami et al. (2015) — What Actually Teaches Critical Thinking** Further Reading entry (the evidence) in the AI Toolkit.

**Notes:** Drafted 2026-06-02. Fills the "we say critical thinking but don't *show* how" gap — the existing CT-adjacent challenges (wk6, wk14, wk21) are all *defensive* (don't let AI erode your thinking); this is the *offensive* use (use AI to strengthen your reasoning). Grounded in Abrami et al.'s meta-analysis finding that dialogue, authentic problems, and mentoring are what move CT skills (g+ = 0.30). Pairs with [[whats-yours-to-keep]].
